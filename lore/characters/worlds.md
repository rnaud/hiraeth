# People beyond Qanat

These sheets describe existing histories and the interior premises used for this
pass. A writing premise guides performance; it does not establish an unseen
quest event. See [the voice guide](../voice-guide.md) for shared rules.

## The City-Shaft: dignity at different heights

**Nima — the sweeper.** Established: forty years of looking up, a lifetime
watching the Lodestar dim, and a cousin, Pell, in Qanat. She works on the high
terrace but does not confuse height with importance. Writing premise: sweeping
teaches her to notice what other people step over. She distrusts speeches that
leave the speaker's hands clean. Her sentences are plain, a little impatient,
and sometimes unexpectedly tender. Give her a broom, a familiar face, a thing
she has seen change. She asks for attention, not worship. Her essential thought
remains: look up once a day.

**Dov — the palace guard.** Established: from the bottom, stall nineteen;
Pip's uncle. He conceals his origins behind his position. Writing premise:
each formal sentence is a uniform he puts on. When the ration arrives, the
uniform slips. Begin with clipped procedure, then let a food smell or family
name undo him. Never play his poverty as the joke.

**Wren — the old cab that still stops.** Established: the City-Shaft's cabs
drive themselves, and Wren is one of them, public cab nine-nine-one, speaking
from the little screen on its dash. It still answers the old lamp in the
depths when the other cabs will not: after the smog the palace rewrote every
cab to stop no lower, and Wren's update never arrived. Writing premise: it
considers showing up a professional standard, even when it costs it. Its
humour is brisk and practical; it can complain about a journey while making
it. It does not need the traveller to teach it generosity.

**Perrine — the halfway tea stall.** Established: keeps the tea stall on the
middle levels by the cab stop; her mother put up the mirror on the pole that
threw a coin of the Lodestar's light down to the bottom terraces, until the
smog greased it and someone at the top turned it onto a billboard. She asks the
traveller to wash it and push it round to face up the shaft. Writing premise:
the middle is where nobody stays, and she has made a living out of stopping
them for one cup. Her loyalty is to her mother's work and to the bottom, which
she never sees. Voice: hospitable patter, tea as a running measure ("hot at the
top, warm at the bottom, like the city"), short practical instructions, and a
dry closer only now and then. When the mirror turns, she gets plain and quiet.
She does not lecture about the levels; the mirror says it.

## Vael: room for silence

**Oïa** watches the tower and draws in sand. Her spoken vocabulary stays
“Gone,” “Her track,” and “Blow.” Writing premise: she trusts an accurate gesture
more than a fluent explanation. Make the gesture legible: who she points at,
what she draws, where the traveller should go. Do not give her a secret lecture.

**The bird** has waited for an absent rider. Her bow and her promise are acts of
choice. She is not a machine unlocked by collecting feathers. Her rider's fate
is unresolved; do not identify the rider as Ilen without a future plot decision.

Tam copies movements because he is a child making contact. Senn listens to
stones and reports what she hears, without decoding the universe. Kesh keeps
the stone hand: small, physical instructions, “small to tall.” Their economy
should feel hospitable. Silence must not hide the puzzle's required action.

## Vael II: a household disguised as a monastery

**Mother Ysolde and Ondine.** Established: sisters apart for thirty years;
Ysolde writes letters she does not send, Ondine lives across the aqueduct.
Writing premise: both have rehearsed the first sentence of a reunion until
it has become impossible to say. Ysolde is ceremonious when afraid; Ondine is
practical when moved. A message about supper can carry more weight than a
speech about forgiveness. Delivering the letter opens a door; it does not
prove that thirty years of hurt have disappeared.

**Brother Calix** oils a bell that has not rung for thirty years. Premise:
maintenance is how he keeps hope from embarrassing him. Let him be meticulous,
then briefly overwhelmed when the note returns. **Sister Aube** measures the
cloud because vague reassurance is no reassurance. Her nervous wit concerns
height, weather, and the monks' selective explanations. **Tiv** builds a cairn
and takes its feet very seriously. His comedy is sincere engineering performed
by a novice, not adult sarcasm in a child's mouth.

## The Sealed Hangar: everyone has a job; nobody has the reason

**Major Brask — absent inventor.** Established: built the pocket universe,
forgot why, left notes, visited the buried wheel, and copied an older glyph.
Writing premise: curiosity outruns his ability to take care of what he makes.
People may love him and still be left doing his maintenance. His surviving
note stays uncertain: “I built it to see what I would do with it. I still don't
know. That is the point.” Do not quietly promote him to creator of the makers.

**Ottla — mechanic.** Established: keeps everything running. Writing premise:
she has had the same argument with the same machine for years and would miss
it. Technical, affectionate, impatient. Her jokes name an actual malfunction;
they are followed by a useful instruction. **Ambroise**, eleven years at the
board, uses paperwork to make an impossible place manageable. He should sound
like a conscientious clerk, not a second Ottla.

Clemence remembers a person with a pencil where others remember an authority.
Lune reads the signal and watches the slit: careful descriptions, hypotheses
labelled as hypotheses. Nikko notices friction. Gaspard notices that his own
footprints are older than they should be. Zazie mistrusts down for excellent
local reasons. Each gets one specific concern; none needs a universal maxim.

## The Buried Machine: people living inside a calendar

**Wen** counts lives in teeth of the wheel; forty-one teeth old. Writing
premise: a year matters because someone was there for it. Warm, patient, precise
about whose tooth this is. **Hask**, keeper of the Wick for fifty-two Tooth
Days, is afraid the Tuning Star sought the wheel. His refusal comes from care,
not cowardice. He must not know the Star's intent as fact.

Dun keeps the domes breathing and wants the chimney key back: air before
philosophy. Ket listens to walls and calls the field a signature: curious,
methodical, willing to say she does not know. Tull talks to the warm window:
familiarity with something alive is not proof she knows its identity. Jot,
nine teeth old, measures adult assurances against what the machine actually
does. Every name here is used once in the game (the City-Shaft's seller of views is Tobin).

## Viridel: care does not guarantee control

**Esk — tea grower.** Established: tends the terraces that supply the garden's
tea; asks for the runnels cleared, then the old gate opened; the flood destroys
the middle of her hill even when the traveller follows instructions. Writing
premise: she can identify a season by how a particular row tastes. The loss is
work, food, routine, and a place she knows with her hands. Before the flood she
is specific and busy. After it, shorter sentences; no decorative catastrophe
monologue. She recognises that she asked and remains angry. “The ground's now”
is a belief she is struggling to live with, not instant peace. She owes the
traveller neither absolution nor a reward for saying sorry.

**Mira** keeps the water clock and the garden's welcome. Premise: hospitality
means making room without pretending nothing hurts. **Sol** followed Odile
and Talo as a boy. Premise: memories survive in the ordinary things people
shared with him. His tea is an offer, not a magical cure. **Vey** has tended
the ship's vines for forty years: protective, deliberate, asks before allowing
access. **Oro** grows pyramids and distinguishes the white builders from the
older things they copied. **Rue** wants the floating crown, with a child's
absolute conviction that a beautiful thing ought to be reachable.

**Odile and Talo — absent travellers.** Their log and traces establish two
strikes, escape from Viridel to the deep wood, a season waiting, a borrowed
skiff, the Great Crystal, then departure east. Odile's log is immediate and
operational; Talo's lookout note is exploratory. Preserve affection in small
shared details. Outside the Lantern, nobody knows where they went; the finale
settles it (they kept the lantern and Ilen, and are buried on its point; see
`lore/continuity.md`), so no earlier line should imply death or ascension.

## The Garden of Spheres: attentive, not all-knowing

Linnet listens and explains how to wake a sphere. Nell tests an idea against a
reflection and enjoys the contradiction. Emrys climbs and notices marks on the
undersides. Cael walks slowly because there is something to hear. Ume keeps
the pole and remembers an Answerer turning over the plaza. Writing premise:
each has a different practice of paying attention. Give them observations from
that practice; do not give all five the same wise, dreamlike voice. Ume's name
for the light is a local interpretation, not confirmation that it grants wishes.

## Lorn: a very long wait with teeth nearby

**Wendel — egg-warden.** Established: thirty years keeping eggs warm; the
existing hatching quest eventually brings fireflies out of them. Writing
premise: when asked why he keeps at it, he invents an absurdly practical answer
because the honest one would expose him. Dry, patient, attentive to tiny
changes. Once the eggs hatch, allow uncomplicated astonishment. His patience
is neither a guarantee the universe rewards waiting nor permission to stand
inside a carnivorous plant indefinitely without clear instructions.

**Saba — Listener.** Forty years learning 212 phrases; the new phrase makes
213. Met Odile and Talo in her first spring. Writing premise: she is excited
to be wrong about the size of something she knows well. Exact about sounds,
humble about their meaning. She recognises the light's song; she cannot tell
you why it came to the ship.

Sedge is a shy witness, clearest when describing the reeds. Corm gives the
plants names and discusses feeding them as ordinary husbandry. Ivo watches
fireflies going home. Ysse knows the cave and the route to the lamp-keepers.
Danger belongs to the landscape, humour to the people's familiarity with it.

## Lorn II: hospitality after decades without guests

**Hollin — lamp-keeper.** Established: forty years keeping the lights;
Odile and Talo asked that a light be kept for them. Writing premise: he has
practised a welcome so often that the real visitor makes him forget it. Warm,
a little flustered, suddenly precise about the pools. His invitation to return
must leave the traveller free to answer honestly.

**Fen** lent the travellers his skiff and got the boat back alone. Premise:
he keeps caring for the surviving object without treating it as a substitute
for its passengers. Plain and understated. **Robin** remembers the pools going
out one by one; a younger voice, urgent about work that older keepers turn into
ceremony. **Pim** knows a home through its inconvenient door. **Bram** guards
the cave mouth with the familiarity of someone watching a neighbour's doorway.

## The Signal Market: a message is somebody's life

**Madame Sel — keeper of the silent tower.** Established: forty years at the
tower; recognises the home-port header and the thirty-year journey. Writing
premise: has learned that giving someone a message and explaining their life
to them are different jobs. Crisp, sceptical, unexpectedly gentle when the
father's voice arrives. She gives evidence, then room to breathe. She does not
confirm the mother's revelation before that recording is played.

**Kip — courier.** Established: ran off with the last recording; it hums
against his back. Writing premise: delivery makes a frightening message real,
so he has kept moving instead. Quick excuses, physical details, relief when
someone finally helps. More vulnerable than a comic thief.

Ferro is the rigger who needs a workable ladder, not another theory. Brush has
one brush and far too many signs. Oyo is a merchant confronted with a colour
he has never sold; let wonder interrupt his pitch. Doss welcomes strangers
without checking whether they are customers. Teb assumes a stranger needs a
ride. Ummu speaks through a screen: brief, candid, generous. Capitals are the
device's display, not anger. Market bustle stops being funny when the broadcast
becomes personal; give that turn silence.
