# A world worth talking to

The reference blend is the readable adventure and village warmth of Ocarina of
Time, the spacious visual imagination of Moebius, and the bodily, sacred,
impossible machinery of visionary science fiction. Use those broad qualities to
make Hiraeth’s own voice. Nobody needs to quote another work to belong here.

## The conversation contract

Every conversation should give the player at least one of these: a person to
care about, something useful to try, or a specific question worth pursuing.
Give necessary directions before making their meaning mysterious.

A good quest exchange contains a local problem, a personal reason it matters,
and a next action the player can repeat in plain language. “The well is dry.
Our festival needs its water. Enter the giant’s mouth beyond the back gate.”
The giant is the wonder. Finding the gate should not require interpreting poetry.

Use short sentences and concrete nouns. Usually one idea per dialogue page;
aim for roughly 15–45 words, shorter for repeat hints and children. The engine
already divides conversations into pages: avoid changing the number of pages
casually, especially in timed scenes. Directions should name a destination,
landmark, verb, and any necessary order. Repeat a useful name rather than “it”
when two objects are in play.

Use local vocabulary, then translate it once. The Wick is a lamp. The oculus is
the open-topped drum. The Hush is one culture’s name for the familiar glyph.
Keep the names that make the world distinctive without asking players to learn
five unexplained nouns in one breath.

## Humor that belongs to somebody

People take their own work seriously even when their world is absurd. The joke
is a potter evaluating a miraculous container, a courier paid in pears, a clerk
with no procedure for a universe going wrong. Avoid modern internet jokes,
self-aware quest commentary, and every character ending every page with a gag.

Nour teases to settle frightened people. Marrow jokes to manage his reputation.
Calix jokes because hope embarrasses him. Ambroise jokes without realising how
absurd his regulations sound. These should not become interchangeable voices.

Leave entire beats without a joke: a frightened witness, the mark on a second
wreck, Esk naming her lost rows, the broadcast, the parents’ stone. A laugh just
before a quiet scene can help; a punchline that cancels it does not.

## Mystery has evidence

A witness reports what they saw before explaining what they believe. The light
turned; three lamps went out; a compass now points toward the ship. Theories
belong to speakers. “I think it was searching” is different from the narrator
confirming a search. No new speech should declare what the Singer really is,
why it drains the ships it passes, or what happened to Ilen.

Let cultures disagree about meaning while agreeing on observable events. Keep
the glyph’s many names. Vary the witness accounts: a compass failure, flowers
turning at night, a bell ringing without a clapper. Repeating “a wet finger
round a glass” everywhere makes different witnesses sound like one author.

Not everyone knows the protagonist is grieving. Avoid supernatural insight from
ordinary musicians, children, or shopkeepers. They may notice tiredness or
homesickness; they do not deliver the ending’s lesson ahead of schedule.

## What the player says

The traveller is curious, decent, sometimes defensive, and able to be funny. He
asks the practical question the player is likely asking. Do not make all choices
lyrical, or make politeness the only available personality. Existing choices
and consequences stay as built in this pass.

Vael is the exception to normal sentence density. Oïa keeps her three spoken
utterances: “Gone,” “Her track,” “Blow.” Give her clear gestures. Other locals
can use a few concrete words. Silence is communication, not a puzzle about
what the writer meant.

## Recordings and home

The recordings never answer the current traveller. They contain old domestic
remarks found by a search word; their accidental relevance is what hurts.
The ship reports dates; the son supplies interpretation. Early messages may be
harsh. Later warmth must not establish that the earlier hurt was imaginary.

The father’s love and his harmful way of expressing fear coexist. The mother
can understand him without absolving him. Tove can welcome the traveller and
still expect him to show up for Lou. Lou has her own projects and complaints;
she is not a tiny therapist or a prize for finishing enough worlds.

Esk does not owe forgiveness because the player meant well. The apology can be
sincere while the damage remains. The revised recording asks the son to help
when wanted and respect a request to leave; it does not prescribe departure as
the universal answer to causing harm.

## Text conventions

Preserve `~tone~` tags; the voice system uses them. Parenthesised action and
narration are silent. Use `*short emphasis*` for actionable objects and places.
Long starred spans are voiced quotations, so use them for actual recordings or
inscriptions rather than ordinary directions. Preserve `{glyph}` and template
placeholders. Do not rename identifiers, rewrite condition strings, change
speaker metadata, or move effects while editing prose.

Controls must match the current build. Retain Xbox / PlayStation names where a
controller hint is needed. Keep instructional UI plain; reserve character
humor for the character’s own voice. Recheck adjacent player choices after any
NPC rewrite: the answer still has to answer the question the player selected.
